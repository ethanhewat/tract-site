Why 75% of Furniture Purchases Start With a Google Search

By the time someone walks into your showroom, they've usually already decided who they trust. That decision gets made on a screen, weeks before it gets made on your shop floor.

Published 30 August 2026

Three-quarters of furniture purchase decisions involve a Google search before the buyer ever contacts a store. Not because furniture shopping has moved entirely online — most people still want to sit on a sofa before they buy it — but because the research phase that used to happen by driving between showrooms now happens on a phone, in bed, over a few evenings.

Understanding what that research phase actually looks like changes how you should think about your website, not just whether you rank for it.

The search happens in stages, not one moment

Nobody searches "buy sofa" once and converts. The typical furniture buying journey involves several distinct types of search, spread over days or weeks:

  • Broad category research — "corner sofa styles," "solid oak dining table" — the buyer is still forming taste and budget, not ready to buy from anyone yet.
  • Local intent search — "furniture showroom near me," "[town] furniture shop" — the buyer has decided to visit somewhere in person and is choosing who to visit.
  • Brand and reputation checks — searching a specific showroom's name to check reviews before making the trip, often after seeing an ad or a recommendation elsewhere.
  • Product-specific search — searching an exact product or a close variant, usually the final step before contacting a store or visiting.

Each stage is a different opportunity to be visible, and most independent showrooms only show up, if at all, for one of them — usually their own brand name, which does nothing to bring in anyone who hasn't heard of them yet.

Local intent is where most showrooms lose the buyer

"Furniture showroom near me" and its local variants are some of the highest-intent searches in the entire buying journey — the person has already decided to visit somewhere today or this week. This is also where a lot of independent showrooms are invisible, because they've never properly set up or maintained the basics: a complete, accurate Google Business Profile, consistent name/address/phone details across the web, and location-specific pages on their own site rather than a single generic "contact us."

Losing visibility at this stage doesn't mean the buyer doesn't visit a showroom — it means they visit a competitor's.

Reputation gets checked whether you manage it or not

A large share of local searches for a specific business are reputation checks — someone who's already interested wants to see reviews before committing time to a visit. If your review profile is thin, outdated, or scattered across platforms nobody's actively watching, you're relying entirely on word of mouth to survive a moment where you had every opportunity to control the narrative.

What this means practically

Being visible across this whole research phase isn't one task — it's several, and they compound:

  • Technical foundations — a site that loads fast, works properly on mobile (where most of this research happens), and is structured so Google can understand what you sell and where.
  • Local visibility — a complete, active Google Business Profile and location-relevant pages, not just a homepage that mentions your town once.
  • Product and category pages — real, indexable pages for what you sell, not just a PDF catalogue or a gallery of images with no text Google can read.
  • A managed reputation — reviews actively gathered and visible, so the "brand check" stage works in your favour instead of being left to chance.

None of this replaces paid channels or reactivation — it works alongside them. But unlike ad spend, which stops the moment you stop paying, organic visibility compounds. A page that ranks well keeps bringing in research-stage buyers for years after it's built, at no incremental cost per visitor.

The mistake is treating it as optional

Most independent showrooms either ignore SEO entirely, assuming it's too slow or too technical to bother with, or treat their website as a digital brochure that exists but was never actually built to be found. Both leave the majority of the research phase — the part where buyers are actively deciding who to trust — entirely to competitors who did the work.
